Tensor compression

My Julia experiment starts with a noisy array of 30,720 numbers. A Tucker decomposition brings that down to 616. In one run, standard SVD reconstructed the array with 1.98% relative error. Randomized SVD gave 3.38% at the same size.

English elevator pitch

Speaking notes for the 2–3 minute video.

Hi, I’m Ahmed. I study maths and computer science at ENSEEIHT. One bit of maths I keep coming back to is approximation. If I can solve the smaller version of a problem, I want to know what’s left of the original and which details I’ve lost along the way. I like taking those questions from the page into a piece of code I can run and check.

In a first-year project, a teammate and I worked on compressing a three-dimensional array. I later made a small version of the experiment in Julia with generated data. It started with over 30,000 numbers. Both methods got that down to 616. What interested me was that they used the same amount of storage, but one reconstructed the input more accurately. That gave me a concrete way to talk about the trade-off.

I’d like to do more of that in scientific software. At EPFL, Daniel Kressner’s work gives me ideas for trying different ranks and measuring their cost. At Manchester, Nicholas Higham’s work makes me want to test what happens when the arithmetic gets less precise. Stanford’s Summer Session is another possibility. I’d like to learn more about matrix computation there and understand the algorithms behind the SVD that Gene Golub helped develop. The common thread in all three ideas is finding a question I can turn into an experiment and explain clearly to someone else.
